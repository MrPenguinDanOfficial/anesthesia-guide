Understanding Anesthesia

A complete, plain-language guide

Anesthesia

an·es·the·sia  /ˌanəsˈθiːʒə/  · from Greek an- “without” + aisthēsis “feeling”

Anesthesia is the medical practice of safely switching off pain, awareness or movement so that doctors can perform surgery and other procedures. It is one of the most important inventions in the history of medicine, and today it is also one of the safest.

01

What anesthesia is

Anesthesia means using medicines to create a temporary, reversible state in which a person does not feel pain during a medical procedure. Depending on the type, the person may also be unconscious, unable to remember, and unable to move.

Doctors describe a complete general anesthetic as having four goals:

  • Unconsciousness (hypnosis): you are not awake or aware.
  • Pain control (analgesia): your body’s pain signals are blocked.
  • No memory (amnesia): you do not remember the operation.
  • Stillness (immobility): your muscles are relaxed so the surgeon can work safely.
In simple words

Anesthesia is not normal sleep. In normal sleep, a loud noise or a pinch would wake you. Under general anesthesia, nothing wakes you until the doctor stops the medicine.

02

How it works in the body

Your brain and nerves work by sending electrical and chemical signals. Anesthetic medicines interrupt those signals in different places.

In the brain (general anesthesia)

Most general anesthetics, such as propofol and anesthetic gases, boost the brain’s main “calming” chemical messenger, called GABA. This slows down brain activity until the parts of the brain that create awareness stop communicating with each other. Ketamine works differently, by blocking a messenger receptor called NMDA.

Scientists still do not fully understand how consciousness is switched off. It remains an active area of research.

In the nerves (local and regional)

Local anesthetics such as lidocaine block tiny gates on nerve cells called sodium channels. Without those gates, a nerve cannot send its signal. The message “this hurts” never reaches the brain, even though you are fully awake.

Analogy

Think of your nerves as telephone wires and your brain as the phone. Local anesthesia cuts one wire for a while. General anesthesia turns off the phone itself, so no calls get through from anywhere.

The four types of anesthesia

The type chosen depends on the operation, your health, and sometimes your preference. Many operations combine two types, for example general anesthesia plus a nerve block for better pain control afterwards.

Fully unconscious

General anesthesia

Medicines given through a vein (IV) or breathed in as a gas make you completely unconscious. A device is usually placed to protect your breathing.

Medicine is injected near a group of nerves to numb a whole area. Includes spinal, epidural and nerve blocks.

Yes, or lightly sedated

Local anesthesia

An injection, spray or cream numbs only the small area being treated.

Used for

Sedation

Medicine relaxes you and reduces awareness. Often combined with local anesthesia. Also called “monitored anesthesia care.”

Used for
Colonoscopy, cataract surgery, some scans
Awake?
Partly, depending on depth

05

Step by step: a general anesthetic

Pilots often compare anesthesia to a flight: the most important moments are the takeoff and the landing.

  1. Hours to weeks before

    Pre-operative assessment

    The team reviews your health, medicines, allergies and past surgeries, examines your airway, and explains the plan. You sign a consent form.

  2. In the operating room

    Preparation

    An IV line is placed in your hand or arm. Stickers for the heart monitor, a blood-pressure cuff and a finger clip for oxygen are attached. You breathe pure oxygen through a mask for a few minutes to fill your lungs with a safety reserve.

  3. About 30 to 60 seconds

    Induction (“takeoff”)

    The induction drug goes into your IV. Many people feel a cool or stinging sensation in the arm, then fall asleep quickly. Children often fall asleep by breathing gas through a mask instead.

  4. Just after you fall asleep

    Securing the airway

    Because anesthesia can stop you from breathing on your own, the team protects your airway with one of three devices: a face mask, a laryngeal mask airway (a soft cuff that sits above the voice box), or an endotracheal tube (a tube placed through the vocal cords into the windpipe). A ventilator can then breathe for you.

  5. The whole operation

    Maintenance

    The anesthesia provider stays with you the entire time. They adjust the gas or IV medicines minute by minute, give fluids, manage pain and blood pressure, keep you warm, and respond to anything the surgery causes.

  6. Last 10 to 20 minutes

    Emergence (“landing”)

    The anesthetics are turned off and reversal drugs are given if needed. As you begin to breathe on your own and respond, the breathing device is removed, often before you remember anything.

  7. Usually 30 to 90 minutes

    Recovery room

    Nurses in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) monitor you, treat pain and nausea, and decide when you are ready to go home or to a hospital ward.

Canadian and American standards require continuous monitoring of oxygen, breathing, circulation and temperature during every anesthetic. These are the numbers on the screen at the top of this page.

Why this matters

When pulse oximetry and capnography became standard in the 1980s, deaths from anesthesia fell sharply. The machine now warns the team of a problem seconds after it begins, often before it can cause harm.

07

The anesthesia team

Anesthesiologist

A medical doctor who completes medical school and then five years of specialty residency, certified in Canada by the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons. They lead the anesthesia plan and are experts in emergencies, airway management, pain medicine and critical care.

Anesthesia assistant and nurse anesthetist

In Canada, anesthesia assistants (usually respiratory therapists or nurses with extra training) help the anesthesiologist. In the United States, certified registered nurse anesthetists (CRNAs) also give anesthesia independently in many states.

The ASA physical status classification

Before surgery, the team grades your overall health. A higher class means the team will plan more carefully, not that surgery cannot happen.

08

Before and after surgery

Before

  • Fasting. An empty stomach lowers the risk of stomach contents entering the lungs. Typical rules: no solid food for about 6 to 8 hours, and clear fluids (water, apple juice, black tea) until about 2 hours before. Always follow your own hospital’s instructions.
  • Medicines. Bring a full list, including vitamins and herbal products. Some blood thinners and diabetes medicines must be paused.
  • Tell the team about allergies, loose teeth, snoring or sleep apnea, and any family member who had a bad reaction to anesthesia.
  • Stop smoking as early as possible. Even a few days helps your lungs.

After

  • Expect grogginess for several hours. It is normal to feel tired the next day.
  • Do not drive, use machines, drink alcohol, sign legal papers or make big decisions for at least 24 hours.
  • Have an adult take you home and, ideally, stay with you the first night.
  • Start eating slowly with light foods and drinks.
  • Call your doctor if you have trouble breathing, chest pain, a fever, or nausea that does not stop.

10

History of anesthesia

Before anesthesia, surgeons had to work as fast as possible while patients were held down. Alcohol, opium and herbal mixtures dulled pain only a little. The discoveries below changed medicine forever.

1799

English chemist Humphry Davy notices that nitrous oxide (“laughing gas”) relieves pain and suggests it could be used in surgery.

1842

Dr. Crawford Long in Georgia uses ether to remove a tumour from a patient’s neck but does not publish his work until years later.

1844

Dentist Horace Wells uses nitrous oxide for tooth extraction. His public demonstration fails when the patient cries out.

1846

On October 16, dentist William Morton gives ether at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston while a surgeon removes a tumour painlessly. The day is still celebrated as Ether Day.

1847

Scottish obstetrician James Young Simpson introduces chloroform.

1853

Dr. John Snow gives chloroform to Queen Victoria during the birth of Prince Leopold, making anesthesia for childbirth socially accepted.

1884

Austrian eye doctor Carl Koller uses cocaine drops to numb the eye, the first modern local anesthetic.

1898

German surgeon August Bier performs the first spinal anesthetic.

1942

In Montreal, Dr. Harold Griffith and Dr. Enid Johnson use curare as a muscle relaxant during surgery, a world first that transformed anesthesia.

1943

The Canadian Anaesthetists’ Society is founded, with Harold Griffith as a founding leader.

1956

Halothane, a safer non-flammable gas, enters use and replaces ether and chloroform.

1980s

Pulse oximetry and capnography become standard. Propofol is introduced in 1986.

Today

Ultrasound-guided nerve blocks, brain-activity monitors and new reversal drugs such as sugammadex make anesthesia more precise than ever.

“CA” marks a Canadian milestone.

Myths and facts

Anesthesia is just a very deep sleep.

It is a controlled, drug-induced state. Brain activity looks very different from sleep, and you cannot be woken by noise or touch.

Many people wake up in the middle of surgery.

True awareness is rare, and the team monitors for signs of it throughout the operation.

The anesthesiologist leaves once you are asleep.

A qualified anesthesia provider stays with you from the start to the end of the anesthetic.

You might not wake up.

For healthy patients, serious complications are extremely rare. The medicines are short-acting and stop working soon after they are turned off.

If you had a bad reaction once, you can never have anesthesia again.

Most reactions can be avoided next time with different medicines. Tell your team about your history.

12

Common questions

Will I dream under anesthesia?

Some people report dream-like experiences, usually as they fall asleep or wake up. Most people remember nothing at all between falling asleep and waking in recovery.

Why can’t I eat or drink before surgery?

Anesthesia relaxes the muscles that normally stop food from coming back up. If the stomach is full, food or acid could enter the lungs and cause a serious lung infection called aspiration pneumonia.

Why do I have to take off nail polish?

Some dark polish can interfere with the finger clip that measures oxygen. Your nails and lips also help the team see your colour.

Is anesthesia safe for children?

Yes. Children’s hospitals have anesthesiologists trained specifically for babies and children. Parents can often stay with their child until they fall asleep.

How long does it take to wake up?

You usually start waking a few minutes after the medicines are turned off. Feeling fully clear-headed can take several hours, and tiredness may last into the next day.

Can I choose the type of anesthesia?

Often, yes. For many operations there is more than one safe option. Ask your anesthesiologist to explain the choices and the pros and cons of each.

Glossary
